# All things in their place #
The four photon analysis is coming along swimmingly. The categories and channels for this search are fairly well defined now. The analysis is basically split between mass symmetric and asymmetric categories. Within each of these categories we are dividing events into bins of either well-reconstructed four photon events, and those we suspect of having at least misfired photon. 

For the symmetric category I will soon have basically everything that would be needed for preapproval, I'm just waiting on the last of the Run 3 simulation to wrap up. The mass asymmetry category will take some additional time, but is moving along along well. I've submitted the signal requeset for that. Once I have sufficient samples from the mass asymmetric cateogry I can begin checking out best cuts for different parts of that phase space. Once I have full preliminary limits for everything, we will finally get this "show on the road" so to speak. Within a month or so I expect the symmetric channel to be ready, with the asymmetric following not too far behind. 

There isn't much to share in terms of the thesis project progress though.

## The Bard's Tale (A2)
On one hand, one may feel this is skipping ahead in the progression of CRPGs a tad. On the other, I just felt like fully playing it.

For the uninitiated, *The Bard's Tale*, is a CRPG about as classic as it gets. It is your classic 6-person party on a quest to defeat an evil sorcerer jaunt. However, while it does have a rough early game, it does iterate on much of what I found lacking in the earlier Ultima and Wizardry games. 

![](https://i.imgur.com/QhVnkRE.png style="width:50%")


To begin, the class selection offered is more in line with what Ultima 3 had offered, making it have more build variety than Wizardry. Most classes are meaningfully different from each other, and I can't think of any that are outright unviable on an individual basis. Although you might not want a party of 6 thieves. While, a possible sore point is that completion of this game does require at least some use of a bard. That should be expected given the game's namesake though. 

Combat execution is roughly in line with what you would expect at the time. It essentially operates on a *for* loop, where one must wait for damage to be dished out to each target individually. This is not normally a problem, unless you're farming fights with 400 Berserkers (30 minutes is a while to wait).

Perhaps one of the biggest boons in BT's favor is the much more reasonable progression system, compared to its compatriots. Ultima's flat curve is easily cheesed, making for a rather short experience earlier on. Wizardry's exponentially increasing curve, makes leveling past L13 nigh unobtainable. However, Bard's Tale actually has a viable route past L13, while also having reasonable experience curves. 

Another boon for this game is its implementation of Armor Class and a Spell Resistance stat. In earlier CRPGs, while high AC did help reduce your chance of being hit it didn't really matter nearly as much as it should have. You can have the best AC attainable in Wizardry and still get hit and die very easily. AC is much more reasonable here. The additional of a Spell Resistance stat also helps mitigate getting hit with statuses and death. I only wish it were more readily communicated. 

Possibly the more interesting aspect of BT is that statuses can be worse than death. While death at late game is readily curable, statuses like STONE and OLD do not have associated spells to cure. You must trek all the way out to a Temple. It's certainly a choice. 

Healing and Leveling are a bit strange in this game. By any convention, one would expect resting at an inn would restore your HP and/or Spell Points. However, no such system exists here. Only temples, spells, or songs can heal you. Spell Points are restored either passively when in town or with specific gear. Otherwise you are expected to go pay for them to be restored, which is the preferable option late game. To level you are required to go to a *Review Board* that judges you. I feel like this is some sort of joke about corporate. 

The actual map and dungeon design I quite appreciate. This is a game that expects the player to fully engage with it and respects the player's intelligence. Exploring this game to its fullest felt rewarding. My only gripe here is that it could have used more NPCs, as its storytelling and worldbuilding are almost exclusively through exploration of the dungeons and town.

I was also quite appreciative of the art direction in this game. The picteresque nature of the dungeons and animated sprites really add a character to this game. While Ultima 3 started pushing in the direction of 3D rendered dungeons, I felt Bard's Tale was still a step up in these terms. 

One of the few gripes I do have though is that often times bosses in this game, such as Mangar, are massive pushovers compared to regular encounters. 

All in all though, I feel like this is where you can begin seeing the genre fleshing itself out as viable. This may have been an archaic experience devoid of some aspects I prefer to see in an RPG, but still worth experiencing. I'm giving this a **5.8/10**. My understanding of the genre will certainly be more complete by the time I've completed Ultima IV and Might and Magic 1, so I will likely return to a conversation about these games. 

## Wild Arms (PS1)
As I have alluded to in previous posts, this was one of those games that accidently flew under my radar. Now that I've finished it, I can say that I did enjoy my time with it. 

*Wild Arms* is a JRPG set in a near apocalypse, following a trio of three characters fighting to save what's left from being exterminated by demons. The premise itself is quite novel, the gameplay is pretty standard. 

![](https://i.imgur.com/HP5ZKiE.png style="width:50%")

In terms of how this game played, I could say it plays slightly above average. This is a classic turn-based RPG, with most battles you can just mash through without much thought. That being said some strategy is required for specific bosses. In fact, there's a plethora of hidden/optional challenge bosses to choose from should you seek them out. 

World exploration leaves a bit to be desired. While you do get a ship and eventually an airship as well, the map itself is a bit confusing. There being no labels on the map you can access only makes matters worse, given how much back and forth you must do. 

With the exception of the last dungeon, most dungeons are short jaunts. This game employs the use of mazes, and tool-based puzzle solving. While playing this I found myself reminded of more recent titles like *Sea of Stars*. Color me shocked when this game makes reference to a 'Sea of Stars' by the end. That said, this game suffers from the same issue of many dungeons being forgettable due to being short little jaunts. While this game also has reused bosses, it is largely offset by the large number of bosses that aren't.

![](https://i.imgur.com/4r72861.png style="width:50%")


While I was worried about character progression around the midpoint, these concerns were alleviated. There is a solid proportion of this game where your skills and spells peak, with no progression in sight for awhile. However, this eventually remedied with you get access to all of the advanced spells and abilities in the late game. The balance in the end-game however can feel a bit hand-fisted. 

One aspect of this game in terms of both gameplay and artistry I felt was underexplored was the 3-character split. The initial impression this game gave was that it wasn't necessarily a 3-character party JRPG, but a 3 protagonist title. While this is sort of true, 90% of its runtime it is in fact a 3-character JRPG. While they developed the mechanics to split the party up more often, they underutilize this in both the narrative and game mechanics. Better execution of this mechanic beyond just puzzle solving would have elevated the game. 

Speaking of characters, let's think about the writing. The playable cast, while appearing simple at first are quite well written. I found them quite enjoyable even. The supporting cast are good as well. Sometimes this game even nails its interpretation of a desolate, hopeless wasteland of a world. Unfortunately, this is hampered by the cartoon cutouts the demons are at times. Not all are written equally, some have depth while others are just comically evil. Oftentimes, this game comes off more as a Saturday Morning Cartoon, wherein the playable cast is just fighting off their villain off the week. After which, they will get away proclaming, "I'll get you next time!". Sometimes this is a bit too cliche for its own good. 

Of course, as is tradition and base requirement for any JRPG, the artistry counts where it counts. The art direction is good and honestly a bit unique. The sprites are endearing and I quite like them, they sort of remind me of some of Quartet's work. The music is also pretty solid, doing a good job of selling the Western vibe they're trying to give. 

I would say this was a pretty good game, but doesn't quite match up to what later came out on the PS1. I'm giving it, a **7.5/10**.

## Ultima III: Exodus (A2 and NES)
While I have not quite beaten this yet, I believe I've played enough to get the jist. The early Ultimas thus far have been really rough jaunts. However, this one is definitely the best out of the original trilogy. 

![](https://i.imgur.com/zZyL4od.png style="width:50%")


The basic premise of Ultima III follows the first two. Mondain and Minax created a spawn (*Does this count as a Title IX violation? /s*) of evil called Exodus, who is the new evil terrorizing Sosaria. The four heroes of Ultima III are essentially setting out on a quest to take Exodus out. 

The first major difference is that this is now a multiple character quest, like Wizardry and Bard's Tale. This game has 4 characters you control rather than 6, not unlike what Final Fantasy did later on. With this expanded selection comes expanded responsibility. That's more food to manage, and more levels and money to grind out. 

This game also has a completely revamped combat system, which is basically all about positioning. The PC version essentially plays like perhaps the earliest version of Real-Time With Pause (RTMP), where the pause can be executed by bringing up the stats menu (at the expense of that character's turn). The NES version by comparison plays as a traditional turn-based RPG. 

The quality of world exploration is roughly the same as the prior two games. You still need to fight pirates for a boat to travel the world. The NPCs have the same depth as in Ultima 2. 

Levelling in this game functions roughly the same it always has, except that to level past L5 you must engage in dungeon exploration to get marks. Increasing your stats however is much improved over Ultima 2. You just have to go to Ambrosia, and pay at the Shrine dedicated to the stat you want to increase. This fixes issues I had with the first two games. You're not going to completely arbitrary waypoints to cheese stats for free, and you're not wasting money to fail to level the stats you want to increase at a random hotel.

Dungeons have also been greatly improved. This may be one of the first games with dungeons with fully rendered geometry. Dungeons in this game are also fully handcrafted. They are also a little necessary to beating the game, fixing one of my major gripes with Ultima 2. One aspect I do miss from Ultima 1 though is the existence of additional side quests. However, the NES version at least adds one with th e flower collection. 

Perhaps one of the biggest boons is that this game sheds some of the worst game design and exuction from the first two games. The very poorly executed space travel in the first two games is nowhere to be seen thankfully. There is also no more an expection that you need to randomly know to kill jesters and guards to beat the game. In fact this game gives you all the information you need to beat it, just by talking to all the characters in its world. 

Overall, I would consider Ultima III a massive improvement over the first two. In fact, I really feel like it is starting to come into its own here. It's just not quite there yet. Somewhere between Ultima III/IV and Bard's Tale Is where a well realized rendition of tabletop on PC came to be for sure. I'm giving this one a **5.5/10**.

P.S. To be frank, the NES version is probably the better one here in my experience. The encounter rate is much  more reasonable, making the early game go by much quicker. You're not really losing out on any content here, and in fact get more!
